01
Hedge Fund Strategies
Long/Short · Global Macro · Arbitrage · Event Driven · Quant
New ▾
India's Category III AIF (Hedge Fund) universe crossed ₹1.8 lakh crore in AUM in 2025, driven by institutional allocations and growing HNI demand. Unlike mutual funds, HFs use leverage, shorting, and derivatives to pursue absolute returns regardless of market direction. Mastering their strategies is prerequisite for any alternative investment career.
Core Hedge Fund Strategy Families
1
Equity Strategy
Long/Short Equity (L/S)
The most widely used HF strategy globally. A fund buys (goes long) undervalued stocks and simultaneously sells short overvalued ones. Net exposure varies from market-neutral (0%) to directional (60–100% net long). India L/S funds typically run 40–80% net long with 100–150% gross exposure. Profit comes from the spread between winners and losers — stock selection alpha, not market beta. Key metrics: Sharpe ratio, Information Ratio (IR), hit rate on longs vs. shorts.
2
Macro Strategy
Global Macro
Trades across currencies, rates, commodities, and equities based on macroeconomic views. A fund might short the Indian rupee while longing gold if it expects a current account crisis. Uses futures, swaps, and options for liquid, levered expression. Pure macro funds often run 5–10x leverage. India-specific macro plays include RBI rate policy, monsoon-driven commodity moves, and EM capital flow cycles. Discretionary macro relies on judgment; systematic macro uses quantitative signals.
3
Relative Value
Statistical Arbitrage & Pairs Trading
Exploits price discrepancies between related securities. Classic pairs: long HDFC Bank, short ICICI Bank when their spread widens beyond historical norms. Stat arb on NSE uses mean-reversion signals across cointegrated pairs, sector ETFs vs. constituents, or futures vs. spot basis. Indian cash-futures arb yields 6–10% annualised before costs but requires high-frequency execution. Convergence trades can fail if fundamentals diverge — "the spread can stay irrational longer than you stay solvent."
4
Event Driven
Event-Driven & Special Situations
Profits from corporate events: mergers, delistings, restructurings, rights issues, open offers. Merger arb buys the target and shorts the acquirer post-announcement, capturing the spread between offer price and market price. India-specific plays include SEBI open offer arb (26% mandatory tender), demerger plays, and NCLT resolution arbitrage in IBC cases. Event-driven returns are asymmetric — high probability of small gain, small probability of large loss on deal break.
5
Quantitative
Systematic / Quantitative Strategies
Algorithmic execution of signals derived from price, volume, fundamentals, and alternative data. CTA (Commodity Trading Advisors) use trend-following across futures. Factor-based quant funds exploit Fama-French factors: value, momentum, quality, size, growth. India quant universe is growing: NSE tick data, exchange co-location, and F&O liquidity allow institutional quant. Common pitfalls: overfitting to historical data, factor crowding during market stress, regime shifts invalidating models.

Key Terms
Alpha
Return in excess of what a benchmark or factor model would predict. Pure alpha is uncorrelated with market moves — the holy grail of HF investing.
Beta
Sensitivity to market moves. A fund with beta 0.5 moves half as much as the index. Market-neutral funds target beta near zero.
Gross Exposure
Sum of absolute value of longs and shorts. A fund 100% long and 40% short has 140% gross exposure.
Net Exposure
Long minus short positions as % of AUM. Measures directional market bet. Net 0% = market neutral.
Short Interest
Total shares sold short as % of float. High short interest (above 10%) can create "short squeezes" when stock rallies force short-sellers to cover.
Quick Check
A fund is ₹120 long and ₹50 short on ₹100 AUM. What is the net exposure?
Net Exposure = (Long - Short) / AUM = (₹120 - ₹50) / ₹100 = 70%. The 170% answer would be gross exposure = (120 + 50)/100.

02
Options & Derivatives
Greeks · Payoffs · Volatility · Strategies · VaR
New ▾
India's NSE is among the world's largest derivatives exchanges by volume. F&O turnover exceeds ₹500 lakh crore monthly — dwarfing cash market volumes. Hedge funds use options for directional bets, hedging, and volatility trading. Understanding Greeks is non-negotiable for any HF risk role.
Option Payoff Profiles
Call Option (Right to Buy)
  • Long Call: Pay premium, profit if stock rises above strike. Max loss = premium. Unlimited upside.
  • Short Call: Receive premium, profit if stock stays below strike. Max profit = premium. Unlimited loss risk.
  • Break-even = Strike + Premium paid
  • Used by HFs to get leveraged upside with defined downside
Put Option (Right to Sell)
  • Long Put: Pay premium, profit if stock falls below strike. Max loss = premium. Max gain = Strike - 0.
  • Short Put: Receive premium, profit if stock stays above strike. Used in cash-secured put strategies.
  • Break-even = Strike - Premium paid
  • Used by HFs to hedge long book or bet on downside

HF Option Strategies
📈
Covered Call
Own stock + sell call. Generates income (premium) from existing long positions. Caps upside. Common in India's yield-enhancing HF mandates on blue-chip holdings.
🛡️
Protective Put
Own stock + buy put. Portfolio insurance. Pay theta every month for downside protection. Used by HFs ahead of binary events: elections, earnings, policy decisions.
↔️
Straddle / Strangle
Buy call + put at same/different strikes. Profit from big move in either direction. Used around earnings or budget announcements. Long vol strategy — expensive in India's high-IV environment.
📊
Iron Condor
Sell OTM call + put, buy further OTM to cap losses. Profits when stock stays range-bound. Popular in high-vol environments — sell inflated premiums, collect theta. Max profit if Nifty stays in range.
Implied vs. Realised Volatility
India VIX = Implied Vol of Nifty 50 index options (market's fear gauge) Volatility Risk Premium = Implied Vol - Realised Vol If IVol > RVol → options are expensive → sell volatility If RVol > IVol → market moves exceed expectations → buy volatility Historical India VIX range: 10–85 (peaked during COVID March 2020) Budget/election premium: IVol typically 25-40% above normal 2 weeks before
Value-at-Risk (VaR) Basics
📉
Parametric VaR
Assumes normal distribution. VaR (1-day, 99%) = Position × Vol × 2.33 / √252. Fast to compute. Underestimates tail risk — ignores fat tails and skewness in Indian markets.
🔁
Historical VaR
Replays actual past returns on today's portfolio. Captures real market events: COVID crash, demonetisation shock. Limited by history — doesn't predict novel stress scenarios.
🎲
Monte Carlo VaR
Simulates thousands of random return paths based on input parameters. Most flexible — can model correlations, fat tails, and option non-linearities. Computationally intensive but used by institutional HFs.
⚠️
CVaR / Expected Shortfall
Average loss beyond the VaR threshold. If 1% VaR = ₹10L, CVaR asks: "on the days it's worse than ₹10L, what's the average loss?" Better tail risk measure — preferred by SEBI for Category III AIFs.
Quick Check
You buy an ATM call option when IVol = 30% and Realised Vol has been 18%. This trade is most likely a...
IVol (30%) significantly exceeds Realised Vol (18%) — the Volatility Risk Premium is +12%. Buying options here means paying expensive implied vol. A vol seller would benefit if realised vol stays below 30%. This is actually a bad time to be long vol on a pure IV basis.

Risk Management Frameworks
Drawdown · Leverage · Factor Exposure · Position Sizing · Stress Testing
New ▾
Risk management separates institutional hedge funds from retail speculation. Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM) lost $4.6 billion in 1998 despite Nobel laureates running the models — because leverage amplified what seemed like rounding errors. In India's HF space, the top risk metrics are: drawdown, leverage, factor concentration, and liquidity-adjusted VaR.
Drawdown Analysis
Max Drawdown = (Peak NAV - Trough NAV) / Peak NAV × 100% Drawdown Duration = Time from peak to recovery Calmar Ratio = Annualised Return / |Max Drawdown| A Calmar > 0.5 is considered acceptable; > 1.0 is strong Example: Fund peak ₹150, trough ₹112 → Drawdown = (150-112)/150 = 25.3% If annualised return = 18%, Calmar = 18% / 25.3% = 0.71
Risk Classification Matrix
🔴 Tail Risk
Extreme Move Events
3-sigma+ moves, flash crashes, systemic crises. Black Monday 1987 (-22% in a day), COVID Mar 2020 (-13% in single session on NSE). VaR models fail here — CVaR and stress tests are essential.
🟡 Factor Risk
Systematic Factor Exposure
Unintended bets on value, momentum, or quality factors across positions. During factor crowding, correlated blow-ups across seemingly diversified books. Fama-French decomposition reveals hidden factor tilts.
🟡 Liquidity Risk
Forced Unwind / Margin Calls
Inability to exit positions at fair value under stress. India small-cap liquidity dries up in crises — 10% of daily volume per day rule to avoid market impact. Margin calls force fire sales, amplifying losses.
🟢 Operational Risk
Process & System Failures
Trade errors, system outages, settlement failures. Knight Capital lost $440M in 45 minutes (2012) due to a software bug. Mitigated by pre-trade checks, kill switches, and dual-approval processes.
Leverage Mechanics
Leveraged Return = L × r_stock − (L−1) × borrow_rate Where: L = leverage ratio, r_stock = stock return, borrow_rate = prime broker rate (typically 8–10% in India) At 4× leverage: L=4, borrow_rate=8.5%, stock_return=15% Gross Return = 4 × 15% = 60% Funding Cost = (4-1) × 8.5% = 25.5% Net Leveraged Return = 60% - 25.5% = 34.5% At 4× leverage with -10% stock loss: Gross Loss = 4 × (-10%) = -40% Funding Cost = 25.5% Total Net Loss = -40% - 25.5% = -65.5% on AUM
Margin Call Mechanics
1
Prime Broker
Initial Margin Requirement
When taking on leverage, the prime broker requires initial margin — typically 20–25% of position value. A ₹100 position at 4× leverage on ₹25 of own capital: initial margin = 25%.
2
Trigger
Maintenance Margin & Margin Call Price
If the stock falls and equity drops below the maintenance margin (usually 20%), the broker issues a margin call. Margin Call Price = Loan Amount / (Position × (1 - Maintenance Margin%)). At 4× leverage on ₹100 stock: loan = ₹75, margin call at ₹75 / (1 × 0.80) = ₹93.75 — only a 6.25% drop triggers it.
3
Crisis
Forced Liquidation
If margin is not met within hours, the broker force-liquidates positions. The fund must add cash or reduce positions under market stress — precisely when prices are most unfavorable. Liquidation cascades can infect other positions in the book as the fund rushes to raise cash.

Quick Check
A fund has ₹100 AUM, borrows ₹300 (4× leverage). Maintenance margin is 20%. Below what stock price (from ₹1000 entry) would a margin call occur?
Loan = ₹300. Position = ₹400 (at ₹1000 price). Margin Call Price ratio = Loan / (Position × (1 - MaintMargin)) = 300 / (400 × 0.80) = 0.9375. Stock margin call price = 1000 × 0.9375 = ₹937.50 — only a 6.25% drop triggers it at 4× leverage.

Fund Structure & Fees
AIF Cat III · 2&20 · Hurdle Rate · High Water Mark · SEBI Regulations
New ▾
India's hedge funds operate as Category III AIFs under SEBI's AIF Regulations 2012. Minimum investment is ₹1 crore per investor. The "2 and 20" fee structure (2% management fee, 20% performance fee above a hurdle) creates significant alignment — but also agency problems if not structured correctly.
India Hedge Fund Regulatory Structure
1
SEBI AIF
Category III AIF Registration
All Indian hedge funds must register as Category III AIFs with SEBI. Minimum corpus: ₹20 crore. Minimum investor commitment: ₹1 crore (₹25L for employees/directors). Category III AIFs can take leverage, short sell, and invest in derivatives — unlike Category I/II AIFs which have restrictions.
2
Structure
Legal Structure: Trust + Manager
Indian AIFs are structured as trusts. The fund has a Trustee (usually a corporate trustee) and an Investment Manager (the fund management company). SEBI requires the Investment Manager to have minimum net worth of ₹5 crore. Key Personnel (fund managers, CRO, compliance) must be disclosed and approved.
3
Leverage
Leverage Limits
SEBI allows Cat III AIFs to take leverage up to 2× NAV for listed equities via F&O. Overseas borrowing is not permitted. Prime brokers (Indian entities: Kotak Securities, IIFL, HDFC Securities) provide margin funding for equity leverage. Repo/TREPS used for short-term liquidity. Leverage reporting required monthly to SEBI.
4
Tax
Pass-through Tax Treatment
Category III AIFs do NOT enjoy pass-through tax treatment — unlike Cat I & II AIFs. The AIF itself is taxed at the fund level at maximum marginal rate (42.7% for trusts). This is a structural disadvantage vs. foreign HFs investing through Mauritius/Singapore treaty routes, though recent amendments partially address this for specific categories.

Performance fee only on returns above the 8% hurdle rate. The performance fee is ₹320 = 20% × (24% - 8%) × ₹10,000 = 20% × ₹1,600 = ₹320. At 8% hurdle, gross must exceed 8% before the manager earns any performance fee.
High Water Mark (HWM)
High Water Mark = Previous highest NAV at which performance fees were charged Year 1: NAV ₹100 → ₹120. Perf fee charged. HWM = ₹120. Year 2: NAV ₹120 → ₹105. Loss year. HWM remains ₹120. No perf fee. Year 3: NAV ₹105 → ₹118. Still below HWM. No perf fee. Year 4: NAV ₹118 → ₹135. Crosses HWM ₹120 → perf fee on ₹135-₹120 = ₹15 excess. HWM protects investors: managers must recover losses before earning perf fees again. Some funds have "loss carryforward" provisions — perf fees never charged on prior losses.
Key Terms Glossary
Hurdle Rate
Minimum return that must be earned before performance fees kick in. Typically 6–10% in India. May be "soft" (fees on all returns once hurdle crossed) or "hard" (fees only on returns above the hurdle).
High Water Mark
The highest NAV at which performance fees were previously charged. Prevents managers from earning fees multiple times on the same profit. Investors get loss recovery protection.
Lock-up Period
Minimum holding period before investors can redeem. Typically 1–2 years for Indian AIFs. Allows managers to deploy into illiquid positions without forced selling. Followed by quarterly/annual redemption windows.
Clawback
Provision requiring fund managers to return performance fees if subsequent losses erode gains. Rare in India's AIF structures but growing in institutional mandates. Aligns long-term incentives.
Carried Interest
Performance fee share allocated to the fund manager as a % of profits. "20% carry" means manager keeps 20¢ of every ₹1 profit above hurdle. In India, taxed as business income at fund level.
Side Pocket
Segregated account for illiquid or hard-to-value positions. Allows the liquid portfolio to continue operating while distressed assets are wound down separately. SEBI allows side pockets for Cat III AIFs in specific circumstances.

Quick Check
A fund charges 2% management fee and 20% performance fee with an 8% hurdle and HWM. NAV goes from ₹100 to ₹130. What is the performance fee per unit?
Gross gain = ₹30. Hurdle = 8% of ₹100 = ₹8. Gain above hurdle = ₹30 - ₹8 = ₹22. Performance fee = 20% × ₹22 = ₹4.40 per unit. Net to investor = ₹30 - ₹2 (mgmt fee) - ₹4.40 = ₹23.60, so net return ≈ 23.6% on ₹100 NAV.
