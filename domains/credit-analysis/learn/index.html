Module 01 — Intro to Credit Analysis
📌 What is Credit Analysis?
▼

Credit analysis is the process of evaluating a borrower's ability and willingness to repay debt. While equity analysts ask "how much can this company grow?", credit analysts ask "will this company be able to meet its debt obligations?"

The job has two outputs: (1) a yes/no lending decision, and (2) a price for the risk (interest rate / credit spread). Both depend on the same underlying analysis.

Credit analysis applies to: corporate bonds, bank loans, working capital facilities, equipment financing, and any instrument where the lender's return is capped at coupon but risk is unbounded on the downside.

📌 The 5 Cs of Credit
▼

The classic framework for credit evaluation uses five dimensions:

  • Character — Management track record, governance quality, history of honouring obligations. Hard to quantify, but a red flag here can override everything else.
  • Capacity — Ability to repay from cash flows. Measured by ICR (EBIT/interest), DSCR (cash flow/debt service), and free cash flow generation.
  • Capital — Equity cushion — how much loss can the company absorb before lenders lose money? Measured by D/E ratio and net worth.
  • Collateral — Assets pledged as security. Important for secured lending; determines recovery rate in default scenarios.
  • Conditions — Macro and industry backdrop — where are we in the credit cycle? Is the sector facing structural headwinds?
📌 Credit Ratings — How They Work
▼

Indian credit rating agencies (CRISIL, ICRA, CARE, India Ratings) assign ratings from AAA (highest safety) to D (default). The long-term rating scale:

RatingCategoryWhat It Means
AAAHighest SafetyStrongest capacity to honour obligations. Very low credit risk.
AAHigh SafetyVery low credit risk, marginally below AAA.
AAdequate SafetyLow credit risk but susceptible to adverse economic conditions.
BBBModerate SafetyLowest investment-grade. Adequate capacity under normal conditions.
BB / BSpeculativeVulnerable to economic changes; elevated credit risk.
C / DDefaultAlready in default or on the verge of default.

Each major category (AA, A, BBB) is further split into + and – modifiers. CRISIL and ICRA use identical scales; ratings are solicited (company pays) or unsolicited.

📌 Bank vs. Corporate Credit Analysis
▼

Banks are different from corporates and need a separate analytical lens:

  • Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR) — Basel III requires Indian banks to maintain ≥11.5% (including capital conservation buffer). Higher CAR = stronger buffer against losses.
  • GNPA / NNPA — Gross/Net Non-Performing Assets. A rising GNPA ratio signals deteriorating loan book quality. RBI classifies a loan NPA if overdue beyond 90 days.
  • Net Interest Margin (NIM) — Spread between lending rate and borrowing cost. Higher NIM = more profitable core business.
  • Provision Coverage Ratio (PCR) — % of NPAs covered by provisions. Higher is safer; below 60% raises red flags.
  • Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) — Ability to survive a 30-day stress scenario. Must exceed 100% per RBI rules.

D/E and ICR ratios are not meaningful for banks — use CAR, GNPA, NIM instead.

Module 02 — Leverage & Debt Capacity
⚖️ Gross Debt vs. Net Debt
▼

Gross Debt = All interest-bearing borrowings (short-term + long-term).

Net Debt = Gross Debt − Cash & Cash Equivalents − Short-term Investments.

Net Debt better reflects economic leverage because a company with ₹1,000 Cr debt and ₹900 Cr cash has virtually no leverage. IT companies like TCS / Infosys are "net cash" — negative net debt — because their cash exceeds borrowings.

Use Gross Debt when analysing refinancing risk (can they roll over debt?) and Net Debt for leverage comparisons (how much is really owed?)

⚖️ D/E Ratio — What Levels Signal Risk
▼

Debt-to-Equity = Total Debt / Total Equity (book value). Indicates how much of the business is financed by debt vs. shareholder funds.

D/E LevelSignalTypical Sectors
0.0 – 0.3ConservativeIT, FMCG, cash-generative consumer
0.3 – 1.0ModeratePharma, auto, diversified industrials
1.0 – 2.5ElevatedInfra, utilities, capital-intensive
> 2.5Stress RiskReal estate, deeply stressed sectors

Always benchmark D/E to sector peers — a D/E of 2.0x is normal for power utilities (NTPC, Power Grid) but alarming for an FMCG company.

⚖️ Net Debt / EBITDA — The Analyst's Favourite Metric
▼

Net Debt / EBITDA shows how many years of operating earnings it takes to repay net debt. It's the most widely used leverage metric by rating agencies and leveraged finance desks.

  • < 1.5x — Low leverage; strong capacity for additional debt
  • 1.5x – 3.5x — Moderate; acceptable for most investment-grade corporates
  • 3.5x – 5x — Elevated; typically sub-investment grade territory
  • > 5x — High stress; covenant breach risk in leveraged buyouts
  • Negative — Net cash company; TCS, Infosys, FMCG majors

EBITDA is used instead of EBIT to proxy cash generation before capex — it's not perfect (ignores capex) but allows cross-sector comparison.

⚖️ Interest Coverage Ratio (ICR)
▼

ICR = EBIT / Interest Expense. Measures how many times operating profit covers interest cost. Below 1x = company cannot pay interest from operations.

ICR (EBIT-based) = EBIT / Interest Expense ICR (EBITDA-based) = EBITDA / Interest Expense DSCR = (EBITDA − Capex) / (Interest + Principal)

RBI's CRILC database flags companies as stressed when ICR drops below 1x consecutively. Rating agencies typically expect:

  • AAA: ICR > 20x | AA: ICR > 10x | A: ICR > 5x | BBB: ICR > 2.5x

Rising interest rates compress ICR for floating-rate borrowers — a 200bps rate shock on Reliance Industries' debt load meaningfully changes coverage calculations.

⚖️ Optimal Capital Structure & Tax Shield
▼

Modigliani-Miller (with taxes) shows debt creates value through the interest tax shield — interest is deductible, reducing taxable income. A company paying ₹100 Cr interest at 30% tax saves ₹30 Cr in tax vs. an all-equity firm.

But excessive debt raises financial distress costs: customers avoid you, suppliers tighten terms, and management time shifts to crisis management. The optimal D/E balances these two forces.

In practice, companies target a leverage level consistent with their desired credit rating — typically investment grade (BBB or above). That rating determines their borrowing cost, which feeds back into the capital structure decision.

Module 03 — Credit Scoring & Default Risk
🏅 How CRISIL Scores a Company
▼

CRISIL (and other Indian rating agencies) evaluate five dimensions, each scored 1–5 (1 = strongest):

  • Business Risk (30%) — Industry risk profile, market position, operational efficiency, scale
  • Financial Risk (40%) — Five financial ratios: leverage, coverage, profitability, liquidity, cash flow adequacy
  • Management Quality (15%) — Track record, governance, succession planning, related-party discipline
  • Industry Position (10%) — Market share, pricing power, barriers to entry
  • Liquidity (5%) — Current ratio, cash on hand, unutilised credit lines

The composite score maps to a rating band. A company scoring 85/100 would typically fall in the AA to AAA band. Notch-level decisions (AA vs. AA+) involve qualitative overlays.

🏅 The Altman Z-Score
▼

Edward Altman's 1968 model combines five financial ratios into a single distress score. It was calibrated on US manufacturing firms but remains widely used as a screening tool.

Z = 1.2×X1 + 1.4×X2 + 3.3×X3 + 0.6×X4 + 1.0×X5 X1 = Working Capital / Total Assets (liquidity) X2 = Retained Earnings / Total Assets (cumulative profitability) X3 = EBIT / Total Assets (return on assets) X4 = Market Value Equity / Total Liabilities (leverage) X5 = Revenue / Total Assets (asset utilisation)
Z-ScoreZoneInterpretation
> 2.99Safe ZoneLow distress probability
1.81 – 2.99Grey ZoneElevated risk; monitor closely
< 1.81Distress ZoneHigh bankruptcy probability

For non-manufacturing and banks, Altman developed Z'-Score (private) and Z''-Score (emerging markets). For Indian banks, use CAR + GNPA analysis instead.

🏅 What Drives Rating Upgrades & Downgrades
▼

Common upgrade triggers:

  • Sustained ICR improvement (e.g. from 4x to 8x) over 2+ years
  • Significant debt repayment reducing leverage by 1+ turns
  • Rating agency gaining confidence in management after a change
  • Industry tailwind improving pricing power and margins

Common downgrade triggers:

  • Capex-driven debt surge without proportional EBITDA growth
  • Large acquisition at high leverage — "event risk"
  • ICR falling below covenant levels (2x–3x is typical threshold)
  • Management change with uncertain credit culture
  • Regulatory action, litigation, or fraud

Rating actions typically lag financial deterioration — the market often prices in a downgrade 3–6 months before the official announcement via a widening credit spread.

🏅 IBC / NCLT — The Indian Default Resolution Process
▼

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) 2016 created a time-bound resolution framework for Indian companies. Key features:

  • Trigger — Default of ₹1 Cr+ can trigger admission to NCLT by financial creditor, operational creditor, or the company itself
  • CIRP — Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process. IRP (Interim Resolution Professional) takes over management within 7 days of admission
  • 270-day timeline — Resolution plan must be approved by Committee of Creditors (CoC) within 270 days; extensions possible
  • Liquidation — If no resolution plan approved, company goes to liquidation. Recovery rates: secured financial creditors first, then unsecured, then equity
  • Haircuts — Creditors in large IBC cases (Essar, Bhushan, Jet) have taken 50–80% haircuts. Highlights importance of secured lending and collateral quality
Module 04 — Bond Pricing & Fixed Income
📊 The Bond Pricing Formula
▼

A bond's price is the present value of all future cash flows — periodic coupons plus face value at maturity — discounted at the yield to maturity (YTM).

P = Σ [C / (1+y)^t] + [F / (1+y)^n] C = Coupon payment = Face × Annual Coupon Rate / Frequency y = Periodic YTM = Annual YTM / Frequency F = Face value (₹100 or ₹1,000 for Indian bonds) n = Total periods to maturity (years × frequency)

When market YTM rises above coupon rate → bond trades at discount. When YTM falls below coupon rate → bond trades at premium. At maturity, price always converges to face value.

📊 YTM vs. Current Yield vs. Coupon Rate
▼

Three yield measures, each capturing something different:

  • Coupon Rate — Stated rate on face value. Fixed at issuance. A 7.5% coupon on ₹1,000 face pays ₹75/year.
  • Current Yield = Annual Coupon / Market Price. If same bond trades at ₹950, current yield = ₹75/₹950 = 7.89%. Ignores capital gain/loss at maturity.
  • YTM (Yield to Maturity) = IRR of all cash flows assuming bond held to maturity. The most complete measure. Accounts for price discount/premium amortising to par.

For a discount bond: YTM > Current Yield > Coupon Rate. For a premium bond: Coupon Rate > Current Yield > YTM. At par: all three are equal.

📊 Duration — Measuring Interest Rate Sensitivity
▼

Macaulay Duration = Weighted average time to receive cash flows (in years). A 10-year bond with 7% coupon might have duration of 7.5 years.

Modified Duration = Macaulay Duration / (1 + y/m). The % price change for a 1% (100bps) change in yield.

ΔP/P ≈ −Modified Duration × Δy Example: Modified Duration = 6.5 years, yield rises 100bps Price change ≈ −6.5% × 1% = −6.5%

Convexity adds precision — duration underestimates price rise when yields fall and overestimates price fall when yields rise. High convexity is valuable to bond holders.

Key relationships: Zero-coupon bonds have the highest duration (= maturity). Higher coupon → lower duration. Longer maturity → higher duration.

📊 Credit Spread — Pricing the Risk Premium
▼

Credit spread = Bond YTM − G-Sec (government security) yield of same maturity. It represents the extra yield demanded for taking credit risk over the risk-free rate.

RatingTypical Spread (India, 2024)YTM if G-Sec = 7%
AAA40–60 bps7.4–7.6%
AA+70–100 bps7.7–8.0%
AA100–160 bps8.0–8.6%

Spreads widen in risk-off environments (post-IL&FS in 2018, COVID in 2020) and compress when liquidity is abundant. The OAS (Option-Adjusted Spread) is used for callable bonds to strip out the embedded option value.

📊 G-Sec Market & the Indian Fixed Income Ecosystem
▼

The Indian fixed income market has several layers:

  • G-Secs (Government Securities) — Issued by GoI; risk-free. Benchmarks: 10yr G-Sec yield is the reference rate for all corporate bonds.
  • SDL (State Development Loans) — Issued by state governments; 25–50bps above G-Sec.
  • PSU Bonds — NHAI, REC, Power Finance Corporation. AAA-rated PSUs trade at 40–60bps above G-Sec.
  • Corporate Bonds — Listed and unlisted. Private placement dominates over public issues in India.
  • Commercial Paper (CP) — Short-term (up to 1 year); money market instrument. Rated A1+ to A4.
  • Masala Bonds — Rupee-denominated bonds issued overseas; subject to FEMA and RBI guidelines.

RBI's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) decisions directly move the short end; 10yr G-Sec yields are driven by fiscal deficit, inflation expectations, global rates (Fed), and FII flows.

Interest Coverage Ratio. EBIT divided by interest expense. Shows how many times earnings cover interest cost.
