Bond Price Formula
P = Σ [C / (1+y)^t] + [F / (1+y)^n]

C = Coupon payment (Face × Coupon Rate / Frequency)
y = Periodic YTM (Annual YTM / Frequency)
F = Face value (₹100 or ₹1,000)
n = Total periods to maturity
Bond pricing basics — price, coupon, and YTM relationship ▾
A bond's price is the present value of all future cash flows discounted at the YTM (Yield to Maturity). When market yields rise above the coupon rate, the bond trades at a discount (below face value). When yields fall below coupon, it trades at a premium. A 10% coupon bond priced at YTM 8% trades at a premium — you pay more than face value because you receive above-market coupons. This inverse price-yield relationship is fundamental to fixed income.
YTM vs Current Yield vs Coupon Rate — three very different numbers ▾
Coupon Rate = Annual interest / Face value. Current Yield = Annual coupon / Market price. YTM = Total annualised return if held to maturity (includes capital gain/loss + coupons). A bond with 8% coupon, face ₹1,000, trading at ₹950 has: Current Yield = 8.4% (80/950), and YTM around 8.9% (also factoring the ₹50 capital gain to maturity). YTM is the market's true required return — the number credit analysts use to compute credit spread.
Duration and convexity — how sensitive is this bond to rate changes? ▾
Modified Duration measures price sensitivity: a bond with duration 5 years loses approximately 5% in price for every 1% rise in yields. Convexity accounts for the non-linear relationship — it tells you that the duration estimate overstates losses and understates gains. High-duration bonds (long maturity, low coupon) are the most rate-sensitive. Duration is why a 30-year G-Sec falls 20–25% when yields spike 200bps, while a 2-year T-Bill barely moves.
Credit spread — the market's price for default risk ▾
Credit Spread = Corporate bond YTM minus benchmark G-Sec YTM for the same maturity. A AAA corporate bond might trade 40–60bps above the 10Y G-Sec; an AA bond at 80–120bps; an A bond at 150–250bps. Spreads compress in risk-on markets and blow out in stress. The IL&FS crisis (2018) saw AA NBFC spreads jump from 80bps to 350bps overnight — tripling borrowing costs and triggering a liquidity crisis across the sector.
Fair value vs market price — when should you buy or sell? ▾
A bond's fair value is derived from DCF at the "appropriate" YTM (benchmark G-Sec + credit spread for the issuer's rating). If the market price is below fair value, the bond is cheap — higher YTM than warranted by credit risk. If above fair value, the bond is rich — YTM is low relative to risk. Credit analysts use this to recommend buy/sell on corporate bonds. A deteriorating credit story trading at AAA spread is a sell; a stable credit at AA+ spread is a buy.
