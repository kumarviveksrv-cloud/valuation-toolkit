M&A Deal Mechanics: Why Companies Buy Each Other
M&A is not just a financial transaction — it is a strategic bet on a future that cannot be achieved organically. Understanding the rationale behind deals separates analysts who build models from those who understand them.
Strategic Rationale: The "Why" Behind Every Deal
Every M&A transaction can be traced to one or more of these strategic drivers. Rarely is only one at work.
Scale & Market Share
Combine revenue bases to achieve procurement leverage, spread fixed costs, and become too large for smaller competitors to displace.
Geographic Expansion
Enter new markets faster than organic growth allows. Buying an established player avoids the regulatory, distribution, and brand-building lead time.
Technology & IP Acquisition
Buy the tech stack, talent, or patent portfolio that would take 3–5 years to build organically. Common in pharma, IT, and consumer tech.
Vertical Integration
Acquire a supplier or distributor to control the value chain, reduce input costs, and protect margins against commodity cycles or distribution choke points.
Talent Acqui-hire
Buy a company primarily for its engineering, R&D, or management team. The product may be shut down; the people are the asset being acquired.
Financial Engineering
Acquire a target to unlock tax shields, deploy excess cash on the balance sheet, or improve capital allocation metrics like ROIC and EPS.

The Regulatory Landscape
Indian M&A operates in a multi-regulator environment. The complexity increases significantly for cross-border or listed-company deals.
CCI (Competition Commission of India) — mandatory pre-merger notification when the combined entity crosses asset and turnover thresholds (₹2,000 Cr assets or ₹6,000 Cr turnover). CCI can block, modify, or impose remedies on deals that substantially lessen competition.
SEBI Takeover Code (SEBI SAST 2011) — anyone acquiring 25%+ stake in a listed company must make an open offer for a minimum 26% of the target's shares at the higher of market price or negotiated price. Mandatory tender offers protect minority shareholders.
IBC (Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016) — for distressed acquisitions. Resolution professionals invite bids; the winning resolution plan is approved by the NCLT. Timeline: 180 days (extendable to 330). JSW Steel-Bhushan Power is the landmark case.
RBI & FEMA — cross-border deals must comply with FDI sectoral caps, pricing guidelines, and remittance rules. Regulated sectors (banking, insurance, telecom) require additional regulatory clearances.

Key Insight
The average global M&A deal destroys value for the acquirer's shareholders. Studies show that 60–70% of acquirers underperform their peers in the 3 years post-close. The reasons are almost always overpaying, overestimating synergies, and underestimating integration complexity — not strategic logic. This is why the accretion/dilution test is non-negotiable.

Accretion / Dilution: The IB Analyst's First Test
Before any deck gets written or any synergy gets claimed, the analyst answers one question: does this deal increase or decrease the acquirer's earnings per share? This is the accretion/dilution test — and it is the first analytical hurdle every deal must clear.
What Accretion and Dilution Mean
A deal is accretive when the pro-forma (combined) EPS is higher than the acquirer's standalone EPS. It is dilutive when the combined EPS is lower. The market watches this number closely — a dilutive deal typically triggers an acquirer stock sell-off on announcement day.
// Step 1: Standalone acquirer EPS Standalone EPS = Acquirer Net Income ÷ Shares Outstanding
// Step 2: New shares issued (for stock consideration) New Shares = Stock Consideration ÷ Acquirer Share Price PF Share Count = Acquirer Shares + New Shares
// Step 3: After-tax interest cost (for debt consideration) Interest Cost = Debt Amount × Cost of Debt × (1 − Tax Rate)
// Step 4: Pro-forma net income PF Net Income = Acquirer NI + Target NI + Synergies − Interest Cost
// Step 5: Pro-forma EPS and accretion/dilution PF EPS = PF Net Income ÷ PF Share Count A/D % = (PF EPS − Standalone EPS) ÷ Standalone EPS × 100

The best way to understand accretion/dilution is to walk an EPS bridge — each line shows how the combined entity's EPS is built from the two standalone companies.

The PE Differential Rule
There is a fast mental model: when is a stock-for-stock deal accretive?
PE Differential Rule: In an all-stock deal, the deal is accretive if the acquirer's P/E is higher than the implied P/E of the deal (offer price ÷ target EPS). When the acquirer trades at a higher multiple, it is "paying" with shares that carry more embedded earnings power per rupee than what it is buying.
Breakeven Synergies = (Dilution in EPS × PF Shares) ÷ (1 − Tax Rate) // The minimum synergies needed to make a dilutive deal breakeven
Synergy Payback = Premium Paid ÷ Annual After-Tax Synergies // How many years before the premium is recovered from synergies

Key Insight
The accretion/dilution test is a year-one snapshot, not a long-term verdict. A deal can be dilutive in year one and accretive by year three as synergies ramp up. Conversely, a deal that looks accretive upfront can turn dilutive if synergies fail to materialise or PPA amortisation is larger than modelled. Always check what assumptions the breakeven requires.

Synergies: The Promise vs the Reality
Module 3 of 4
Synergies: The Promise vs. the Reality
Synergies are the most cited justification for paying a premium in M&A — and the most frequently overstated. Understanding how to model, value, and pressure-test synergies is what separates a credible deal from a banker-inflated one.
Types of Synergies
Cost Synergies
Headcount reduction, shared procurement, facility rationalisation, IT consolidation, overlapping vendor contracts. More predictable. 80% of deal synergy value typically comes from here.
Revenue Synergies
Cross-sell, new geographies, bundled pricing, brand extension. Less predictable — customer attrition and execution risk make these the most commonly missed synergy type.
Financial Synergies
Tax shield from debt, improved debt rating, lower borrowing cost at scale, deployment of excess cash. Often overlooked but material in large corporate mergers.
Integration Costs
Severance, system integration, rebranding, consultant fees. One-time costs that offset synergy PV. Often understated by management teams and inflated by integration firms.
The PV of Synergies Framework
// Annual synergy (after-tax, adjusted for capture rate) After-Tax Synergy = Gross Synergy × Capture Rate × (1 − Tax Rate)
// Present value of synergy stream (simplified growing annuity) PV of Synergies = Σ [After-Tax Synergyt ÷ (1 + WACC)^t]
// Payback on the premium Payback Period = Premium Paid ÷ Annual After-Tax Synergies
// Net synergy value (after integration costs) Net Synergy Value = PV of Synergies − PV of Integration Costs
The 50% rule: As a rule of thumb, the acquirer should not pay more than 50% of the PV of synergies as a premium. The remaining synergy value should accrue to the acquirer's shareholders — otherwise, the target's shareholders capture all the value and the acquirer breaks even at best.
Capture Rates: The Missing Assumption
Synergy models almost always show 100% capture. Reality doesn't. McKinsey data across 1,000 deals shows:
Synergy TypeTypical Capture RateWhy it Misses
Cost synergies65–75%Headcount harder to cut than assumed; IT integration costly
Revenue synergies30–50%Customer overlap, product mismatch, channel conflict
Procurement synergies75–85%Most predictable — volume leverage contracts are quantifiable
Integration timingDelayed 6–18 monthsCultural issues, system complexity, regulatory approval time

Synergies don't arrive on Day 1. A realistic ramp-up is critical for accurate year-by-year accretion modelling.

Key Insight
The most dangerous synergy in any deal deck is one that was reverse-engineered from the premium. When a banker works backwards from "what synergies are needed to justify this price," the model passes the test on paper but fails in execution. Always ask where synergy estimates came from — bottom-up operational analysis or top-down premium justification. The answer tells you everything about deal quality.

Deal Structuring: Cash, Stock, Debt and Why It Matters
The financing decision is as consequential as the price decision. The same deal, financed differently, can be accretive or dilutive, investment-grade or junk, tax-efficient or costly. This module covers how structuring decisions ripple through the entire deal analysis.
The Financing Trade-off Matrix
💵 All-Cash
No dilution to acquirer shareholders
Target gets certainty and liquidity
Creates taxable event for target holders
Depletes acquirer's cash/flexibility
Best when acquirer has excess cash + target has low P/E
📈 All-Stock
Target shares future upside
Tax-deferred structure for target (share swap)
Signals acquirer may view stock as overvalued
Dilutes existing shareholders
Best when acquirer P/E > target implied P/E
🏦 Debt-Financed
Interest is tax-deductible (tax shield)
No equity dilution
Increases leverage ratios and credit risk
Covenant restrictions can limit flexibility
Best when target generates strong, stable FCF
Purchase Price Allocation (PPA)
Every acquisition triggers a purchase price allocation under IND AS 103, which requires the acquirer to fair-value all identifiable assets and liabilities. The residual is goodwill.
Allocation StepWhat Gets ValuedP&L Impact
PP&E Step-UpFixed assets revalued to fair valueHigher depreciation charge reduces reported earnings

Collar Structures for Stock Deals
In all-stock or mixed deals, the exchange ratio is set at announcement. Between signing and closing (3–12 months), both share prices can move substantially. A collar protects both parties.
Fixed Exchange Ratio: Target gets a fixed number of acquirer shares. Target's effective price rises/falls with acquirer's stock. Target bears price risk.

Fixed Value (Floating Ratio): Target gets a fixed dollar/rupee value. Exchange ratio adjusts as acquirer price moves. Acquirer bears dilution risk.

Collar: Combines both — exchange ratio adjusts within a band (e.g., ±15%), with fixed ratios at the extremes. Shares risk between both parties.

Key Insight
The deal structure is often more negotiated than the price itself. A target that insists on cash is pricing in risk — they don't believe in the acquirer's synergy story or post-merger performance. A target that accepts stock believes (or is told by their banker) that the acquirer's future price appreciation will compensate them. Watch what form of consideration target shareholders accept — it reveals their conviction in the deal thesis better than any press release.
