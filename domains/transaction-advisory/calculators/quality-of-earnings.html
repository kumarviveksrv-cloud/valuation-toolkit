Four QoE Dimensions

CCR Cash Conversion Ratio

Operating cash flow as a percentage of EBITDA. A ratio above 90% signals high-quality earnings — EBITDA is genuinely converting to cash. Below 70% is a red flag; the gap is absorbed by accruals, receivable build-up, or aggressive revenue recognition.

ACR Accrual Ratio

The proportion of earnings that is accrual-based (non-cash) rather than cash-based. Calculated as (EBITDA − Operating Cash Flow) / Revenue. High accrual ratios flag earnings that may not materialise into cash, signalling manipulation risk or structural collection weakness.

CON Revenue Concentration

Percentage of revenue from the top 1, 3, or 5 customers. High concentration (top customer >20%) is a deal risk — their departure shrinks the business materially. Buyers apply a "key customer discount" to EBITDA multiples in concentrated businesses.

QoE QoE Score (0–100)

A composite score combining cash conversion, accrual ratio, revenue concentration, growth sustainability, and margin consistency. Above 75 = high-quality earnings. 50–75 = moderate risk. Below 50 = significant QoE concern requiring buyer's own diligence overlay.

Quality of Earnings — 5 Key Questions

A Quality of Earnings review is a sell-side or buy-side exercise that stress-tests reported EBITDA to determine how much of it is (a) recurring, (b) cash-backed, and (c) representative of the forward business. Unlike an audit — which verifies that financials comply with accounting standards — a QoE review answers the commercial question: "Is this EBITDA real, and will it continue?" In an M&A deal, the target's EBITDA is multiplied by a sector multiple (7x–15x for Indian mid-market). A ₹10 Cr QoE downward adjustment in a 10x deal destroys ₹100 Cr of equity value. This is why QoE is the highest-ROI exercise in any deal process.

A cash conversion ratio (CCR = Operating Cash Flow / EBITDA) above 90% is generally considered high quality for Indian businesses. IT services companies like TCS and Infosys typically run 85–95% CCR because their business is cash-generative by nature. What causes CCR to be low? Four main drivers: (1) Working capital deterioration — receivables growing faster than revenue. (2) Aggressive revenue recognition — billing before delivery or completion. (3) Large capex disguised as opex. (4) Inventory build-up or obsolescence. A low CCR alone isn't a red flag if the business is growing fast (working capital investment is expected). But a low CCR combined with flat growth is a serious warning that EBITDA quality is poor.

The accrual ratio (Accrual Ratio = (EBITDA − Operating Cash Flow) / Revenue) measures what proportion of revenue is recognised on an accounting basis before cash is actually received. Academic research by Sloan (1996) showed that companies with high accrual ratios statistically underperform in the following 3–5 years — because accruals are less persistent than cash earnings. In a deal context, a high accrual ratio raises questions: Is the company booking revenue on long-term contracts too aggressively? Are there large unbilled receivables (work done but not yet invoiced)? Are provisions being released to inflate EBITDA? Indian construction and IT services companies are particularly prone to high accruals due to milestone-based billing in long-cycle projects.

When a single customer represents more than 20% of revenue, buyers face a binary risk: lose that customer and the business is fundamentally different. The standard approach is a "key customer discount" on the EBITDA multiple — buyers either reduce the multiple by 1–2 turns, or insist on an earnout structure where part of the consideration is paid over 2–3 years conditional on the key customer relationship being maintained. Sellers argue the relationship is contractual; buyers argue contracts don't prevent switching. In Indian B2B and defence companies, government customers often represent 60–90% of revenue — this creates concentration risk even without a single commercial customer dependency, since government budget cycles and policy changes become an outsized risk factor for the combined business.

The six most common QoE red flags uncovered in Indian M&A due diligence: (1) Channel stuffing — revenue spiked in the quarter before the deal was signed, often by incentivising distributors to over-stock. (2) Related-party revenue — a meaningful portion of revenue is from entities owned by the promoter family, which may not survive the ownership change. (3) Deferred revenue reversal — SaaS or prepaid businesses releasing deferred revenue to inflate EBITDA without corresponding service delivery. (4) Provision reversals — bad debt provisions or warranty provisions reversed into P&L with no supportable basis. (5) Export incentive income — government export subsidies counted as EBITDA, which a buyer may not qualify for. (6) Key-man dependency — EBITDA is a function of one person's client relationships; their departure post-deal collapses the earnings base.
