Core NWC Concepts

PEG Working Capital Peg

The agreed reference level of Net Working Capital (Receivables + Inventory − Payables) embedded in deal pricing. If actual NWC at close differs from the peg, the purchase price is adjusted dollar-for-dollar at signing.

LB Locked-Box Mechanism

Price is fixed at an agreed historical balance sheet date. From that date, the seller cannot extract value except through approved "permitted leakage." Simpler and certain — strongly preferred by PE sellers and financial sponsors.

CA Completion Accounts

Purchase price is adjusted post-closing based on actual NWC, cash, and debt at completion. More complex and creates post-closing dispute windows, but gives the buyer economic accuracy. Common in strategic M&A.

SN Seasonality Adjustment

NWC fluctuates significantly by quarter in cyclical businesses. Using a single spot date to peg NWC disadvantages whichever party closes off-peak. A 12-month trailing average eliminates this distortion — the market standard.

Working Capital Mechanics — 5 Key Questions

When a company is sold on a "cash-free, debt-free" basis, the enterprise value assumes a normalised level of working capital is delivered with the business. If the target needs ₹100 Cr of NWC to operate normally but only ₹80 Cr is present at closing, the buyer effectively receives ₹20 Cr less than they paid for. The peg — agreed in the SPA — specifies the normalised NWC level. If actual NWC exceeds the peg, the seller gets a higher price; if it falls short, the buyer gets a credit. A ₹5 Cr peg error in a ₹100 Cr deal is a 5% return impact — significant enough to trigger post-closing disputes and warranty claims.

Locked-box is preferred by sellers: price certainty from signing, no post-closing adjustment risk, simpler documentation. The trade-off is the seller accepts an "economic interest" cut-off at the locked-box date — any cash generated after that date belongs to the buyer, offset by a daily interest accrual (ticker fee). Buyers prefer completion accounts because they get economic accuracy — they pay only for what they receive at closing. However, completion accounts open a window for disputes (what counts as NWC, what is debt-like, is that receivable collectable?). PE sellers almost always push for locked-box; strategic buyers frequently push for completion accounts because they have more integration uncertainty.

Normalised NWC is typically the 12-month trailing average of (Trade Receivables + Inventory − Trade Payables), excluding one-off items. Key adjustments: strip out inter-company balances, exclude disputed receivables over 90 days, remove inventory obsolescence provisions that are management-adjusted, and add back advance payments representing contracted future revenue. The TA team builds a month-by-month NWC schedule for the past 12–24 months to identify spikes or troughs. A rule of thumb: NWC items should be "trading items" directly linked to the P&L. Capex creditors, financial debt repayments, and tax payables are typically excluded. The exact definition is negotiated in the SPA's "Accounting Policies" schedule.

FMCG, retail, and agri-linked businesses can see NWC swing 20–40% between peak and trough quarters. If a deal closes in October (inventory built up pre-Diwali), actual NWC might be ₹80 Cr versus the 12-month average of ₹55 Cr — the buyer pays ₹25 Cr more than the underlying economics warrant. Seasonality adjustments ensure the peg reflects expected NWC at the specific closing date. Sophisticated advisors build a "seasonality band" — upper bound (peak quarter) and lower bound (trough quarter) — and specify expected NWC within that band for each possible close month. This prevents gaming of the closing date by either party and is now standard practice in PE-backed deals.

The five most common NWC disputes in Indian transactions: (1) Receivable ageing — buyers want to exclude anything over 90 days; sellers argue all balances are collectable. (2) Inventory provisioning — sellers want to reverse obsolescence write-downs; buyers insist provisions remain. (3) Debt-like items — unpaid gratuity, deferred revenue, customer advances, government dues: all contested as to whether they belong in NWC or the debt bridge. (4) Tax balances — advance tax, TDS receivables, GST refundable vs. payable: whether these belong in working capital. (5) Related-party items — inter-company balances that net out on consolidation but appear in standalone statements. A detailed, agreed NWC definition schedule attached to the SPA before signing is the only reliable way to prevent these disputes from becoming post-closing arbitrations.
