Due diligence is the systematic investigation of a target company before a transaction closes. It surfaces undisclosed risks — from understated liabilities to overstated revenues — that directly impact deal price, structure, and completion confidence. Poor diligence is the single biggest cause of deal regret: studies show 50–60% of M&A deals destroy value, largely because risks were not identified pre-signing. In India, where promoter-controlled firms are the norm, diligence must go beyond financial statements to related-party networks, regulatory filings, and board minutes.
Revenue diligence (is reported revenue real, recurring, and sustainable?), Cost/EBITDA diligence (are margins inflated by one-time items?), Balance sheet diligence (are assets overstated, liabilities understated?), Legal diligence (are there hidden contractual or litigation risks?), and Tax diligence (are there unprovisioned demands or aggressive positions?). For buy-side, a sixth workstream — commercial diligence — validates the market opportunity and competitive moat independently of management's projections.
Red flags are specific findings that require deal restructuring — price chip, escrow, representations and warranties insurance — or, in severe cases, justify walking away. Common Indian red flags: revenue to related parties exceeding 20% of total, unexplained receivables growth, audit qualifications or auditor changes within 3 years, pending tax demands above 20% of EBITDA, promoter share pledging above 50%, and NCLT / SEBI proceedings. Each red flag gets a severity rating and a corresponding deal structure action — the goal is not necessarily to kill the deal but to price the risk correctly.
Related party transactions (RPTs) can artificially inflate or deflate reported profitability. If a target sells to a promoter-owned entity at above-market prices, or buys from one at above-market rates, the reported EBITDA is misleading. Buyers typically haircut EBITDA for normalised RPT adjustments and may demand that RPTs be wound down or repriced to arm's length before closing. High RPT concentration — above 15–20% of revenue — routinely leads to a 0.5–1.5x multiple discount on EV/EBITDA, and sometimes to an escrow or deferred consideration mechanism to protect against post-closing revelations.
Quality of Earnings focuses on earnings quality — cash conversion, accrual ratio, revenue concentration — all drawn from the P&L and cash flow statement. The Deal Checklist goes wider: it incorporates legal risk (litigation, regulatory), balance sheet quality (audit firm credibility, related party exposure), and tax risk — dimensions entirely invisible in the income statement. Together, QoE plus Deal Checklist give a 360° risk view. A company can have excellent earnings quality but still carry material legal or tax tail risk that derails the deal — the most dangerous transactions are those where the financials look clean but the diligence surface below is compromised.
