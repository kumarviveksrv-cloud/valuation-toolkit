Three modules covering EBITDA normalisation, working capital mechanics, and deal diligence frameworks — the technical backbone of every M&A transaction.

01
EBITDA Normalisation
What adjustments buyers and sellers fight over — and why the normalised number is the only number that matters.
▼

In a transaction, the EBITDA that appears in the target's audited accounts is almost never the EBITDA used to set the purchase price. Between reported and normalised lies a bridge of adjustments — some straightforward, many contested. Understanding this bridge is the first and most important skill in transaction advisory.

Why reported EBITDA is wrong
One-Time Items
Costs or revenues that occurred in the period but will not recur. A factory fire insurance payout inflates EBITDA; a VRS charge deflates it. Both must be removed to see the run-rate business.
Run-Rate Adjustments
Structural changes to the business mid-period: a new plant that ran for 6 months, a price increase effective mid-year. These are annualised to reflect the steady-state earnings power.
Pro-Forma Items
Acquisitions, disposals, or contract wins that weren't in the business for the full period. Pro-forma EBITDA shows what the current business would have earned over 12 full months.
Management Add-Backs
Discretionary costs that belong to the current owners but not to a new buyer — excess promoter salary, above-market rent to a related party, personal expenses run through P&L. Contested territory in every deal.
The EBITDA bridge
Reported EBITDA + One-time costs → removes non-recurring charges − One-time income → removes non-recurring windfall + / − Run-rate items → annualises partial-year changes + Pro-forma items → reflects full-year ownership + Management add-backs → normalises owner-specific costs = Adjusted EBITDA ± Buyer-specific items → removes synergies / integration costs = Normalised EBITDA (the number the deal price is built on)

Who makes the adjustments — and the conflict

Sellers present a management case with the most aggressive adjustments. Buyers commission an independent QoE report and challenge each add-back. The final normalised EBITDA is the outcome of negotiation, not accounting. The spread between management case and buyer case is often 15–25% of EBITDA — meaning a 1× difference in that gap on a 10× multiple deal is a 10–25% difference in enterprise value.

Key insight: The EBITDA bridge is not just an accounting exercise — it is a negotiating document. Every ₹1 Cr of add-back you defend at 10× EBITDA is worth ₹10 Cr of deal value. Transaction advisors who understand the limits of defensible adjustments create more value than those who maximise the add-back list on paper.

02
Working Capital Mechanics
How the working capital peg protects buyers, what locked-box means, and why NWC is the most contentious post-closing adjustment.
▼

Every acquisition effectively transfers working capital from seller to buyer. The question is: how much, and at what value? Getting this wrong is how buyers end up paying for a business they thought was cash-rich but was actually burning cash in the months before closing.

What is working capital in a deal context
Net Working Capital (NWC)
Current assets minus current liabilities, excluding cash, debt, and financial items. The operational float needed to run the business day-to-day. Buyers need it to keep operating from Day 1.
NWC Days Formula
Receivable Days + Inventory Days − Payable Days = NWC Cycle. A business with 60R + 30I − 45P has a 45-day cycle. Every day of NWC ≈ Revenue/365.
The Peg
The agreed "normal" level of NWC that the seller delivers at closing. Typically the trailing 12-month average, adjusted for seasonality. If closing NWC > peg, buyer pays extra; if < peg, buyer gets a refund.
Seasonality Band
Most businesses have seasonal NWC swings. The peg should reflect the average, not the low. Closing in peak season (high NWC) benefits the seller; closing in trough benefits the buyer.
Locked-box vs. completion accounts
Locked-Box (Seller-Friendly)
Price is fixed at a locked date using audited accounts. No post-closing adjustment. Seller retains economic risk/reward between lock date and close. Common in PE deals and competitive auctions.
Completion Accounts (Buyer-Friendly)
Price adjusts post-closing based on actual NWC, net debt, and other items at closing date. Buyer has more protection but process is lengthy — completion accounts can take 60–90 days to finalise.
Leakage (Locked-Box)
Permitted and unpermitted value transfers from the business to the seller between lock date and close — dividends, management fees, related-party payments. Buyer gets a deduction for unpermitted leakage.
Earn-Out Linkage
When NWC risk is high (e.g., a manufacturing company with volatile receivables), buyers sometimes link part of the consideration to post-closing NWC performance over 12–24 months.
How the peg is calculated
Step 1: Collect 13 monthly NWC balances (12-month + opening) Step 2: Average each component (receivables, inventory, payables) Step 3: Exclude non-recurring items (pre-deal costs, extraordinary balances) Step 4: Adjust for structural changes (new products, channel shifts) Step 5: Set peg = adjusted 12-month average NWC Step 6: Define band = peg ± seasonality swing (e.g., ± ₹20 Cr) No adjustment if closing NWC falls within band
Most common NWC disputes in Indian deals
1
Receivables aging — buyer argues old receivables should be excluded; seller argues they are collectable.
2
Inventory valuation — buyer applies haircut for slow-moving or obsolete stock; seller resists.
3
Advance payments — should customer advances be included in NWC or treated as debt-like items?
4
Accrued liabilities — are all expense accruals properly reflected at the reference date?
5
GST / TDS receivables — tax refunds due; buyers include, sellers argue they are cash equivalents.
Key insight: The NWC peg looks like a back-office accounting detail, but on a large transaction it can shift the effective purchase price by 5–10%. Transaction advisors who set a conservative peg (high for buyers, low for sellers) provide their client with substantial economic protection that is invisible in the headline price.

03
Deal Diligence Frameworks
How to structure a buy-side diligence, score risk across workstreams, and translate findings into deal structure adjustments.
▼

Diligence is not a checklist exercise — it is a risk-pricing exercise. The goal is not to find reasons to walk away. The goal is to understand what risks exist, quantify them, and decide how to price or structure around them. Every finding should map to a deal structure implication: chip the price, put money in escrow, demand a rep and warranty, or walk away.

The five workstreams
1. Revenue Diligence
Is the reported revenue real? Is it recurring? Check revenue recognition policies (IFRS 15 / Ind AS 115), contract terms, deferred revenue, and whether sales are front-loaded near the period end.
2. Cost / EBITDA Diligence
Are margins sustainable? Look for costs shifted to capex, related-party rent below market, promoter salary well below or above market, and any expense deferred into future periods.
3. Balance Sheet Diligence
What are the real assets and liabilities? Focus on receivables aging (30/60/90+ day buckets), inventory turns versus peers, off-balance-sheet obligations, and contingent liabilities in notes.
4. Legal Diligence
Pending litigation, NCLT proceedings, SEBI notices, change-of-control clauses in key customer contracts, IP ownership, title to land and plant — all must be verified against original documents.
5. Tax Diligence
Unprovisioned demands under appeal, aggressive transfer pricing positions, withholding tax exposure, GST notices, and MAT credit utilisation assumptions — together these often reveal liabilities the balance sheet understates by 5–15% of EV.

Quality of Earnings — the financial diligence crown jewel
Cash Conversion Ratio
Operating Cash Flow / EBITDA. Should be 80–100%+ for a quality business. Below 70% suggests aggressive accruals or working capital deterioration eating up reported profits.
Accrual Ratio
(EBITDA − Operating Cash Flow) / Revenue. Measures the proportion of earnings that never converts to cash. High accrual ratio = reported earnings exceed economic reality.
Revenue Concentration Risk
Top customer as % of revenue. Above 25% triggers enhanced revenue diligence and often a retention mechanism in the deal structure. Above 50% is typically a deal-shaper.
Sustainability of EBITDA
After all QoE adjustments, does the normalised EBITDA represent a real, recurring earnings stream that a buyer can count on? This single judgment drives the multiple paid.
The diligence process — how it flows
1
Management presentation and data room access — information memorandum, 3-year audited accounts, month-by-month MIS.
2
Financial diligence kick-off — preliminary QoE, NWC analysis, and normalisation adjustments based on data room.
3
Management Q&A sessions — interrogate add-backs, challenge revenue recognition, verify customer contracts.
4
Legal and tax specialists — third-party lawyers review litigation schedule; Big4 tax team review demands under appeal.
5
Diligence report — consolidated findings, risk register, deal structure recommendations, normalised EBITDA with sensitivities.
6
Price negotiation — buyer chips the price for identified risks; seller contests; final price reflects risk-sharing agreement.
Key insight: The best diligence teams are not the ones who find the most problems — they are the ones who correctly prioritise. A high-severity finding missed is catastrophic. A medium-severity finding inflated into a deal-killer destroys value needlessly. The judgment call between "this is priceable" and "this is a walk-away" is where transaction advisory earns its fee.

Transaction Advisory Glossary

EBITDA Bridge
A line-by-line reconciliation from reported EBITDA to normalised EBITDA, showing every adjustment and its business rationale.
Normalisation
The process of adjusting reported financials to reflect the underlying, sustainable economics of the business — stripping out one-time, owner-specific, and non-recurring items.
One-Time Item
A cost or revenue that occurred in the measurement period but will not recur in the ordinary course of business. Must be clearly evidenced and non-recurring in nature.
Run-Rate Adjustment
An annualisation of a structural change that happened mid-period — a new plant, a price increase, a contract win — to reflect 12 months of the changed business.
Pro-Forma Adjustment
A restatement to show what the current business would have earned if an acquisition or disposal had occurred at the start of the measurement period, not mid-period.
Management Add-Back
An expense specific to the current owners — excess salary, above-market RPT costs, personal expenses — that a new buyer would not incur, added back to reach normalised EBITDA.
Working Capital Peg
The agreed reference level of NWC that the seller delivers at closing, typically based on the trailing 12-month average. Price adjusts up or down if actual closing NWC differs from the peg.
Locked-Box Mechanism
A deal structure where the price is fixed at a historical balance sheet date, with no post-closing NWC adjustment. The buyer bears economic risk from the lock date to closing.
Completion Accounts
A deal structure where the final price adjusts post-closing based on audited accounts prepared as of closing date, reflecting actual NWC, net debt, and other agreed items.
Net Working Capital (NWC)
Trade receivables plus inventory plus other current operating assets, minus trade payables and other current operating liabilities. Excludes cash, debt, and financial instruments.
Quality of Earnings (QoE)
An independent report assessing the reliability and sustainability of reported earnings. Covers normalisation, cash conversion, revenue concentration, and balance sheet quality.
Cash Conversion Ratio (CCR)
Operating Cash Flow divided by EBITDA, expressed as a percentage. A CCR above 85% indicates high earnings quality; below 70% suggests material accrual risk.
Accrual Ratio
(EBITDA − Operating Cash Flow) / Revenue. Measures the share of profits that exists only on paper. A rising accrual ratio over multiple years is a significant earnings quality red flag.
Related Party Transaction (RPT)
A transaction between the target and a party connected to the promoters or management — a sister company, a family trust, a promoter-controlled entity. High RPT volumes inflate or deflate reported EBITDA and require normalisation.
